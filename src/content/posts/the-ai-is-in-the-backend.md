There is no mention of AI on my website, in my parent messages, or in any conversation I've had with a family. I use it every working day. Both of those things are deliberate, and they're less contradictory than they sound.

## Nobody is buying AI

Parents are buying a grade in a specific exam, at a specific time, for a specific child. That's the entire transaction.

Put "AI-powered" on a tuition page and you have not made the offer stronger — you've introduced a question the parent now has to resolve before they can trust you. Is a computer teaching my kid? Is that why it's cheaper? Is this person actually going to show up?

The technology is my problem, not theirs. If it works, it shows up as better worksheets and faster feedback, and those need no explanation at all.

> The test isn't whether AI is in the product. It's whether a parent would ever need to know.

## What it actually does

All of it sits behind the lesson:

- **Personalised worksheets.** Once I know a student keeps falling over on, say, ratio questions with a change in one quantity, I can generate a set aimed exactly at that pattern — at the right difficulty, in the phrasing PSLE actually uses — in the time it used to take me to photocopy something approximate.
- **Online worksheets.** Delivered, attempted and captured digitally, so the record of what a student got wrong is a dataset instead of a stack of paper I have to remember to look at.
- **Spotting the gap.** Across attempts, the useful signal isn't the score. It's that the same underlying question type has now failed four times in five weeks while everything around it improved. That's tedious for a human to track across a roster and trivial to surface automatically.
- **The admin around teaching.** Drafting progress notes, summarising a month for a parent update, the scheduling churn. I edit everything that goes out, but I no longer write it from a blank page at eleven at night.

None of that is glamorous. All of it is time that goes back into the actual lesson.

## What I don't let it do

It doesn't teach. The lesson is one-to-one, live, with me, and I have no interest in changing that.

It doesn't decide what a student needs. It proposes; I choose. The gap between "this student got these wrong" and "here is what is actually going on in their head" is still entirely human, and it is the only thing I'm really selling.

And nothing reaches a parent unedited. A generated progress note that is 90% right is not 90% fine — it's a message about someone's child.

## Why backend-only is the right call

The uncomfortable truth about AI in education is that the demos point at the student and the value points at the teacher. Everyone wants to build the tutor. What actually moved the needle for me was removing three hours a week of preparation and record-keeping, which I then spent teaching.

That's the whole strategy. Use it ruthlessly where the student never sees it, so that the forty-five minutes where they do see me is better prepared than anyone else's.

Nobody ever has to know how the worksheet got made.
