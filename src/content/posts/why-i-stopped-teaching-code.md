I spent about five years teaching people to code, across three schools I started myself. I was good at it and I liked it. I quit anyway, and now I teach Primary 5 and 6 math. This is the honest reasoning.

## Teaching code got predictable

The first year is a genuine thrill. By the fourth, you have run the same twelve-week arc so many times you can feel the room's questions arriving before they do. Same syllabus, same build-a-game project at week nine, same parent asking whether their kid should learn Python or Java.

Nothing was wrong with it. That was the problem. There was no pressure making the lessons better, because there was no test at the end that anyone was afraid of.

Coding is bought as enrichment. It sounds future-proof, it looks good in a portfolio, and if a family gets busy in March it is the first thing cut. Nobody has ever called me in a panic about their child's coding.

> A coding class is something a parent buys when things are going well. Math tuition is something they buy when there's a date on the calendar.

## The money follows the exam

I don't think this is only my temperament. The Singapore numbers are unambiguous.

Households here spent about **S$1.8 billion** on private tuition in 2023, up from S$1.4 billion in 2018 and S$1.1 billion in 2013 — a decade of steady growth through a pandemic ([Department of Statistics, via Malay Mail](https://www.malaymail.com/news/singapore/2025/01/20/singapore-families-spent-s18-billion-on-private-tuition-in-2023-with-the-richest-spending-over-s162-per-month/163937)). The average household puts in around S$104.80 a month, and the top-earning fifth of families spend S$162.60 — more than four times what the bottom fifth manage.

Serious money has noticed. Dymon Asia Private Equity backed [Mind Stretcher](https://www.dymonasiaprivateequity.com/investments/mind-stretcher-group/), which runs over 10,000 students across 26 centres. Advent International sits behind The Learning Lab. There is no equivalent roll-up happening in coding schools, and there isn't going to be.

You can see the same gap further down the market, in what small operators ask when they sell. On the local brokerage listings right now, a turnkey robotics and coding centre is going for about S$413,900, while a Chinese enrichment centre in Bukit Timah is listed in the S$1–5 million band ([BusinessForSale.sg](https://www.businessforsale.sg/tuition-and-enrichment-centers-for-sale)). Those are asking prices, not completed deals, so treat them as a signal rather than a valuation. But it is the signal I'd expect: a business attached to a national exam is worth a multiple of one that isn't.

## So why not open a chain

Because that isn't the part I'm good at, and the market already has excellent chains with more capital than me.

My whole bet is narrower. One subject, two levels, taught properly. I'm not trying to be a centre that offers math and science and English and Chinese with a different tutor for each. I'm trying to be the person you go to specifically because it is math, and specifically because the teaching is better than what you'd get somewhere with nicer carpet.

## What "best at math" has to actually mean

It has to mean something you can check, or it's just a claim.

For me that's diagnosis before drilling: finding the specific question types a student is failing, and giving them practice aimed at those, instead of handing them another ten-year series and hoping volume does the work. Everything I build in the background exists to make that diagnosis faster and more accurate.

And it means putting money behind it. If a student sees no improvement in the first month, I refund it. That is a stupid guarantee to offer if you're mediocre, which is rather the point of offering it.

The subject was never the thing I loved. The teaching was. Math is simply where the teaching matters most to the people paying for it.
