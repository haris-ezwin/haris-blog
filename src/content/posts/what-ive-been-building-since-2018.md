I've started or helped run six education businesses in seven years. They look like a scattered CV until you notice that every single one was some version of the same question: how do you teach one person something hard, and then do it again a hundred times without it getting worse?

## I started out writing software

My first job out of the gate was as a software developer. I was good enough at it to become a Google Developer Expert, and that opened doors I didn't expect — I ended up teaching coding at NTU, SMU, ACS(I), MGS and Singapore Sports School.

That was the accident that decided everything after it. I went in thinking I was a developer who taught on the side. I came out knowing I was a teacher who happened to write code.

## Then I built coding schools

Learn App Code came first, then Zenith Coding Academy, then Next Level Academy. Three attempts at roughly the same idea, each one better run than the last.

Then I spent a year as Head of Operations at Sigma School, which was the first time I'd worked on someone else's education business at scale. That year taught me more about the boring half of this industry — scheduling, retention, tutor consistency, the unglamorous machinery — than the three years of founding things before it.

> You can teach a great lesson on instinct. You cannot run a school on instinct.

## Now it's math

I started EzWin Academy in 2024, and by the following year I'd stopped teaching code entirely. It's Primary 5 and Primary 6 math, aimed squarely at PSLE. Online, one-to-one, out of Paya Lebar.

That switch is a whole argument of its own, and I've written it up separately. The short version: coding classes are something parents buy when things are going well, and math tuition is something they buy when there's a date on the calendar. One of those is a business.

## Why this blog

Seven years in, most of what I've learned doesn't fit into a lesson or a sales page. Some of it is about teaching, some is about running a small company in Singapore, and quite a lot lately is about what AI is genuinely good for once you stop trying to put it on the marketing material.

So it goes here. Mostly arguments I couldn't finish in conversation.
